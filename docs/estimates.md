# Estimates against actual time

Jan asked on 22 Nov 2026 that the project keeps what was estimated next to what really took place,
so that the models working with him judge more realistically what fits in a given time. Every
session reads this before it promises Jan a time or a plan with times, and adds to it as it works.
The data is small and honest: a row with no estimate is still useful for the actual, and a number
that cannot be sourced is marked "approx." or left empty.

What it protects is Jan's energy more than his hours. His cost is attention and creative effort,
part of which goes into coordinating the models; available time does not track what he gets done,
available energy does, and it runs out after long rounds (Jan, 22 Nov 2026). So a plan is judged by
how often it brings work back to him as much as by its minutes.

## How to add a row

1. **Write the estimate when it is given**: in a briefing, a plan, an issue's timebox. In minutes,
   with who gave it (in the row: lead, Jan, or the issue). If none was given, write "none given"; do
   not invent one afterwards.
2. **Add the actual when the work is merged or the answer given**, from timestamps and not from
   memory: the pull request's `createdAt` and `mergedAt`
   (`gh pr view <n> --json createdAt,mergedAt,commits`), the CI run
   (`gh run list --branch <branch>`), the subagent's duration as the harness reports it. GitHub
   times are UTC. A number from the harness has no timestamp in the repository, so it is "approx.".
3. **Units are minutes of wall clock.** For built work the span runs from the first commit or the
   delegation to the merge. Commit dates can be later than the pull request's opening when a branch
   is amended; then the opening of the pull request is the start, and the row says so.
4. **Waiting for Jan is its own column**, so it is not blamed on the work. Fill it only when it can
   be read from timestamps; leave "-" otherwise.
5. **One line on what made the difference**: the thing that a later estimate should know.
6. **Count the returns to Jan.** In the same last column, say how many times the work came back to
   him (a question, a preview to look at, a decision): "returns: 2". Each one costs attention
   whatever the clock says. A plan says in advance how many it expects, and batches them.

Kind is one of build, review, verify, decide, docs. Who did it is lead, subagent, Cursor or Jan.
Rewrite "What the numbers say so far" when rows are added.

## What the numbers say so far

The table is thin: 22 rows from one weekend, most with no estimate. What the rows support:

1. **A CI round is about 6.5 minutes, and a pull request that Jan looks at in motion needs at least
   two.** Nine runs took 5m58s to 6m49s (mean 6m29s; the last row). Both #129 and #133 had two
   completed runs on the pull request, and #133 a third that was cancelled by the second push. Even
   a pull request that only changes docs waits one round (#130, #137, #150: 8 minutes).
2. **A subagent on a smaller model builds a well-specified part in 10 to 20 minutes**: the baseline
   in about 12, the sparks in about 18, the footer change in about 12 (#134, #135, #149). Review by
   the lead was 6 minutes for the baseline and 36 for the sparks, where the lead changed the turn
   and the colour and Jan confirmed them. So the review is as long as the build or longer when the
   look is judged in motion, and shorter when it is stills and a script. A fault that shows in 1 to
   2% of runs took a subagent about 20 minutes to reproduce (#155).
3. **Jan's check of a preview brings one or two follow-ups, so plan a second round.** After his
   check of #133 two defects were fixed inside it, and #136 was added (and #135 changed by the
   lead). The two-hour plan was kept, with the unplanned work inside it; by how much is not known,
   because its start was not written down (61 minutes is the part that has timestamps). One plan
   only; it says the follow-ups fit in the plan's slack, not that plans may be doubled.
4. **Long local Playwright walks are not reliable evidence on a loaded machine.** The reading of
   #131 took about 180 minutes, almost all waiting on timeouts, while CI ran the same project in
   under six minutes. The reading of #119 round 2 took about 6 minutes when the three local runs
   passed. Use CI, and give a subagent a limit.
5. **What is delegated to Cursor may not come back the same day, or at all.** #119 took 91 minutes
   from the pull request to the merge in two rounds, #131 218 minutes, the sparks half of #38 was
   not returned and a subagent built it. The time before the pull request is not on record. A
   delegated pull request that Jan looks at took more than one round: #153 had three and 77 minutes
   from opening to merge, against 33 minutes and one round for #154, which he did not need to try in
   motion.
6. **Jan's timeboxes measure his effort, not the elapsed time.** #132 was given 10 minutes and the
   elapsed time was about 395 minutes overnight; #89 stood open for about 21 hours. When a plan
   needs his answer, count the wait as its own line, and do not plan the work behind it to start the
   same hour.
7. **The number of returns to Jan is underestimated when a preview has not been tried on his own
   hardware.** The evening plan for #89 said one return and it took four: the scroll hint did not
   show, twice, and he asked two questions. The footer change (#149) came back once because only a
   keyboard had been tested; a mouse had not. Two plans only; plan for two returns where the thing
   is judged on a device or a pointer the lead has not tried.
